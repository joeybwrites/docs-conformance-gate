# Part 4: Adoption

> Draft for Joey to voice/finalize.

I've built and run a documentation-quality system like this before, at a previous company, so I want to lead with the mistake I made rather than a plan I'm confident about in the abstract. That system was good. Teams that used it found it genuinely useful. It still didn't land internally, and the reason was simple: I made it optional. Because it was a helpful-but-skippable step, the first deadline that got tight was the last time anyone ran it. The part that stuck with me is where it *did* succeed — outside that org, where other engineering leads picked it up and made it a non-optional requirement for all of their teams' outgoing user-facing content. The tool didn't change; the authority behind it did. So my one firm conviction going in is that optionality is the failure mode: a good tool that can be skipped will be skipped, and it only sticks when someone with authority makes it non-optional. The authority that actually works, I've found, is usually the producing team's *own* engineering lead — not a horizontal ask from the docs team that none of these teams report to.

The design in Parts 2 and 3 is built to earn that authority rather than demand it. It has to be useful to the author first, which is why the checker is deterministic, self-serve, and specific: it hands you the exact line and the exact fix, not an opinion. Teams ignore taste, and they're right to — but they don't easily ignore a precise, correct catch that just saved them from shipping something false. On top of that usefulness, I'd shift the same gate left and run it at increasing degrees of bindingness: advisory while someone is drafting, then inside a team's own CI where their lead owns the result, then finally at publish. For any team that engages early, nothing new ever fails at publish, because they already saw it days earlier when fixing it cost ten minutes. And because the standard and the checker are published, versioned artifacts, adoption doesn't require me to push anything sideways — a team, or even their own AI assistant, can pull the current version and check against it as they work. Proliferation just means making the gate trivially droppable into a team's existing pipeline so their lead adopts it as *their* quality bar. That's me borrowing the authority I don't have.

The anchor under all of it is the mandatory publish gate, because that's the one chokepoint the docs/education team legitimately controls — nothing reaches the live site unchecked. That's exactly the thing my previous system never had. And the tiered, non-compensating verdict is what keeps that hard line defensible: hard-block only on the genuinely unshippable, like a page that asserts something false or self-contradictory. "Revise" still blocks, but with a logged, time-boxed override, so docs is never the bottleneck on a judgment call. "Ship with Notes" never blocks. The payoff shows up in the escalation: when a team that ignored all of this hits the wall at publish and takes it to leadership, the framing is "docs stopped us from publishing a false claim" — which wins — and never "docs blocked us over a style nit," which wouldn't.

So the team that ignores me is a case I've already designed around, because I don't need their goodwill. Most teams won't ignore it once their own lead has pulled it into their pipeline, since at that point it's their bar, not mine. The residual few who ignore everything still hit the single chokepoint I do control, and that gate hard-blocks only real errors — so the worst case is bounded and defensible. The authority I don't have gets replaced by two things I do: a chokepoint I control at publish, and borrowed authority earned by making adoption easy and useful everywhere upstream.

One note on scope: richer adoption signals — visibility into which teams ship clean versus getting blocked, as a status and telemetry layer — are the natural next lever, but out of scope for this answer.
