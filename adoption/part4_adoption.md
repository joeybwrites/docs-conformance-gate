# Part 4: Adoption

I've built and run a documentation-quality system like this before, at a previous company, so I want to lead with the mistake I made rather than a plan I'm confident about in the abstract. That system was good. Teams that used it found it genuinely useful. It still didn't land internally, and the reason was simple: I made it optional. Because it was a helpful-but-skippable step, the first deadline that got tight was the last time anyone ran it. The part that stuck with me is where it *did* succeed, outside that org, where other engineering leads picked it up and made it a non-optional requirement for all of their teams' outgoing user-facing content. The tool didn't change; the authority behind it did. So my one firm conviction going in is that optionality is the failure mode: a good tool that can be skipped will be skipped, and it only sticks when someone with authority makes it non-optional. The authority that actually works, I've found, is usually the producing team's *own* engineering lead, not a horizontal ask from the docs team that none of these teams report to.

The design in Parts 2 and 3 is built to earn that authority rather than demand it. It has to be useful to the author first, which is why the checker is deterministic, self-serve, and specific: it hands you the exact line and an actionable reason, not an opinion. I'm not claiming it only ever flags real errors. The checker README is honest that several rules are proxies, and proxies produce false positives. What I'm claiming is narrower and more durable: when it catches something, it points you at the precise spot and tells you why, so you can fix it or knowingly wave it. Teams ignore taste, and they're right to, but they don't easily ignore a precise, correct catch that just saved them from shipping something false. On top of that usefulness, I'd shift the same policy left and introduce it in stages: advisory while someone is drafting, then inside a team's own CI where their lead owns the result, then finally at their publication boundary. For any team that engages early, publish-time failures become earlier, rarer, and less surprising, because they already saw the issue days earlier when fixing it cost ten minutes. And because the standard and the checker are published, versioned artifacts, adoption doesn't require me to push anything sideways. A team, or even their own AI assistant, can pull the current version and check against it as they work. Proliferation just means making the policy trivially droppable into a team's existing pipeline so their lead adopts it as *their* quality bar. That's me borrowing the authority I don't have.

I want to be precise about where this policy actually lives, because it's easy to overclaim here. There is no single universal publish chokepoint that the docs team unilaterally controls, and I'm not going to invent one. Each property, the docs site, the Support center, the platform surfaces, already has its own publication boundary and its own owner. The realistic move is a *common* policy integrated at each of those existing boundaries, which means negotiation and co-ownership with the documentation, Support, and platform owners rather than a decree from us. Concretely, I'd stand it up in stages. It starts report-only, so everyone sees what it would have said without anything being blocked and we can tune the rules against real content. Then it moves to enforcing on changed pages only, so we're never re-litigating the entire back catalog, just what a team is actively touching. Both of those run inside a defined pilot with explicit success criteria and a false-positive budget agreed up front, and promotion to actual blocking enforcement happens only after the pilot's numbers support it. If a rule turns out to be noisy in practice, we demote or roll it back rather than defend it on principle.

That whole path only holds together if ownership is named, not assumed. There's a rule owner who's accountable for the policy content and a registry owner who's accountable for the versioned standard the checker reads from. And there's a documented override process, because a hard line no one can bend is a hard line people route around: any override records who owns it, the reason, an expiration, a log entry, and an escalation path when it's contested. That's what lets docs hold a real line without becoming the bottleneck on a judgment call. The tiered, non-compensating verdict is the other half of keeping the line defensible: block only on the genuinely unshippable, like a page that asserts something false or self-contradictory; "Revise" still blocks but takes the logged, time-boxed override; "Ship with Notes" never blocks. The payoff shows up in the escalation. If an issue reaches the publication boundary, the escalation stays grounded in whether the content is accurate and shippable, not whether docs prefers a particular style.

So the team that ignores this early is a case I've designed around. This approach doesn't require universal initial enthusiasm to work, but I'm not pretending cross-functional adoption comes for free either. It needs legitimate ownership at each boundary and genuine cooperation from the people who own those surfaces, which is exactly why the negotiation, the pilot, and the named owners are load-bearing and not ceremony. Most teams won't ignore it once their own lead has pulled it into their pipeline, since at that point it's their bar, not mine. The residual few who skip everything still meet the common policy where it's been integrated at the boundary their content actually crosses. The authority I don't have gets replaced by two things I can build: a policy that lives at each property's real publication boundary with its owner's buy-in, and borrowed authority earned by making adoption easy and useful everywhere upstream.

One note on scope: richer adoption signals, visibility into which teams ship clean versus getting blocked as a status and telemetry layer, are the natural next lever, but out of scope for this answer.
